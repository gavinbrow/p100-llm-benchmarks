What we found

  • Sparse beats dense, by a lot. Mixture-of-experts models run 3–9× faster than dense models of comparable size. Qwen3.6-35B-A3B is both faster and lower in perplexity than every dense 27B setup. → 2
  • The card runs short of decode compute, not bandwidth. Dense models use 16–65% of the 732 GB/s peak, in order of how costly the quant format is to unpack. A Q3_K file 18% smaller than Q4_0 runs 15% slower. → 3
  • Split every layer across both cards. -sm tensor makes dense models from 8B up 21–44% faster, and every model that loads with it 14–48% faster once the context is full. Five models, Nemotron-3.5-Lightning among them, fail to load with it. → 4
  • One card overheats; two share the heat. Under 25 minutes of load a lone P100 throttles to 906 MHz and loses 25%. Split across both cards, the same model loses 5.5%. Power was never the limit: 118 W of a 250 W allowance. → 4
  • Long context works. Nemotron-3.5-Lightning writes at 50 tok/s with 128k tokens of context, 39 at 256k and 16 at a million, because only 6 of its 52 layers keep a KV cache. With tensor split, five more models write at 28–31 tok/s at 256k. → 5
  • Judge a model at the context you'll use. GLM-4.7-Flash starts at 43 tok/s and falls to 6.8 at 128k. Keep the KV cache at f16: q8_0 costs 21–42% at 72k and saves only 0.2–1.9 GB. → 5
  • Reading the context is the slow part. Filling 256k takes 15 minutes on the fastest model, and a million takes almost three hours. -ub 2048 cuts that by a quarter. About half the models run out of VRAM before their trained context. → 6
  • Agents are practical at 256k. Each turn takes 12–14 s at 250k tokens, because the server re-reads only the ~2,000 new tokens. Four conversations at once give 1.8–1.9× the output of one, and a saved 128k context reloads in 1–9 s instead of 5–27 min. → 7
  • Short drafts win. Speculative decoding pays when the draft is 2 tokens long. Nemotron's built-in MTP head adds 59% on code, and a draft model adds up to 76% to a dense 27B on structured output. A separate draft model makes MoE models slower. → 8
  • Use the CUDA 12.4 build and F16 weights. CUDA 13 dropped Pascal, BF16 reads prompts 1.8× slower than F16, and row split cannot run on these cards. → 1

01

What to run

Run a sparse model. Split it with -sm tensor if it loads, keep the KV cache at f16, and use both cards. Sparse models give 25–110 tok/s here, up to 122B parameters. Dense models of 24B and up manage 4–15, or 6–19 with tensor split.

02

Sparse beats dense

What sets the speed is how many weights each token touches, not how many the model has.

A dense model reads every weight for every token it writes. A mixture-of-experts (MoE) model sends each token through a few experts: Qwen3.6-35B-A3B holds 35B parameters and uses about 3B per token. On a card as short of decode compute as this one (section 3), that decides everything. Mistral-Small-4-119B writes at 39 tok/s, and Llama-3.3-70B, from a file of about the same size, at 4.3.

Active parameters trade against bits per weight. At IQ1_M, the 10B-active Qwen3.5-122B-A10B is a third slower than the roughly 6.5B-active Mistral-Small-4-119B (25 against 39 tok/s). Yet that Mistral is faster than both 3B-active Qwen3-Next models at Q2_K (32). At under two bits a weight, each expert is cheap enough to undercut a model with half the active parameters in a heavier format.

Two that did not load

Nemotron-Super-49B v1.5 (IQ4_XS, 26.9 GB, dense) ran out of CUDA memory under every tensor split from 0.25/0.75 to 0.60/0.40, even under llama-cli with a tiny context: llama.cpp asked for a 17.7 GB buffer on a 16.3 GB card. The model comes out of a neural architecture search and its layers differ in size, which probably defeats a split by layer count. Nemotron-3.5-30B-A3B at Q8_0 is 33.6 GB, more than the 32,574 MiB available.

03

Quantisation: cheap to unpack beats small

On Pascal the fastest quant format is the one that is cheapest to decode, not the smallest file.

A dense model reads every weight once per token, so file size × tok/s is the memory bandwidth it actually uses. Across this study that is 16–65% of the P100's 732 GB/s. Within every model it rises as the format gets simpler to decode. Pascal has no dp4a integer dot-product instruction, so K-quants and I-quants fall back to a slow decode path.

tg128 and pp2048 in tok/s, layer split, 5 repetitions. The tensor-split gain comes from a later run of both modes back to back (section 4). Q8_0 perplexity ran out of memory, because the perplexity tool keeps logits for the whole batch; Q8_K_XL was not measured.

  • Smaller can be slower. Q3_K_XL is 18% smaller than Q4_0 and 15% slower. On a modern GPU the smaller file wins.
  • Q8_0 is the most efficient quantised format, with one int8 multiply to decode. It is slow only because it is twice the bytes. Two unrelated Q8_0 dense models land at 41.6% (phi-4) and 42.1% (Llama-3.1-8B) of peak.
  • The least efficient run is Llama-3.3-70B at Q2_K_XL: 15.8% of peak.
  • Reading speed barely moves with quant format. The quants above read at 170–189 tok/s, a 10% spread against 47% in writing, because prompt processing runs through cuBLAS in FP16 whatever the stored format. Choose a quant for writing speed.
  • MoE models care less. Nex-N2.5-mini is a Q4_K_M, one of the expensive formats, and still writes at 51 tok/s. Sparsity outweighs the decode cost.

04

Two cards: split every layer, and share the heat

Use both cards, and divide each layer across them wherever the model allows.

-sm layerdefault
Each card holds whole layers, and the cards take turns on every token. The second card adds memory, not speed.
-sm tensorrecommended
Every layer, and its share of the KV cache, is divided across both cards, so both work on every token at once.
-sm rowunavailable
The cards report VMM: no, and row split aborts when the model loads.

llama-bench, both cards, f16 KV. "Full context" is 8k–32k for the large dense models (as much as fits) and 128k–256k for the MoE models and Qwen3.5-9B. Full tables below.

On an empty cache the MoE models gain least, because they read so few weights per token. The two exceptions are gpt-oss-20b, up 29% to 80 tok/s, the fastest model above 3B without speculative decoding, and Qwen3.5-122B-A10B, up 18%. With a full context every model gains, because attention over the cache becomes a large share of the work and tensor split divides that too. At 256k, five models go from 21–26 tok/s to 28–31.

It also fits contexts layer split cannot. Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B keeps a KV cache on all 48 layers: with layer split, 128k needed a q8_0 cache and wrote at 6.2 tok/s. With tensor split it held 128k at f16 and wrote at 22.3.

The one cost: reading 2,048 tokens on an empty cache is often slower, by up to 48% on small models. Layer split lets the two cards overlap on consecutive chunks of a long prompt, and tensor split gives that up. That explanation is likely, not measured. Short reads get up to 72% faster.

Heat, not power, is the limit

These are passively cooled datacentre cards in a desktop case, which is how most second-hand P100s end up. Every long run in this study peaked at 80–81 °C, with thermal slowdown active in a quarter to three quarters of the samples. So both cards were tested under sustained load: 25 minutes of back-to-back 512-token generations, started with both cards at or below 45 °C, with telemetry every second.

One P100 doing all the work settles at 80 °C, two degrees under its 82 °C slowdown point (shutdown is 85 °C). It drops from about 1,328 MHz to 906 and loses a quarter of its speed. Split across both cards, each does half the work and the loss is 5.5%. The sparse model reads a tenth of its weights per token and barely warms up. Power was never the constraint: 82–123 W per card of a 250 W allowance, and SW Power Cap never appeared. The MoE also gets about 0.13 tokens per joule (50.8 tok/s at 390 W) against 0.035 for the dense 27B (14.6 tok/s at 418 W), 3.7× the energy efficiency.

One card or two

With layer split, models of 3B and under and gemma-4-26B-A4B are 2–5% faster on one card, gpt-oss-20b is level, and dense models from 8B up are 5–19% faster on two. Much of that lead is heat: on a cool card, Qwen3.8-27B Q4_0 ran 14.74 tok/s on one card against 14.36 on two. With context, one card goes a long way. Qwen3.5-9B Q4_K_M holds its full 256k on a single card in 14.1 GB and writes at 18.6 tok/s, and gpt-oss-20b reaches its 130k window at 35.7. The second card then adds 6–22% to writing and 9–14% to reading.

Why some models hold up

Hybrid models keep a KV cache on only some of their layers; the rest carry a fixed-size state. That sets how much context fits in 32 GB and how much work attention does per token.

128k tokens cost Nemotron 1.75 GB of VRAM and Qwen3.5-9B 5.0 GB, nearly three times as much for a model with a third of the parameters. GLM-4.7-Flash fails a different way. Never choose a long-context model from an empty-cache benchmark.

Big dense models starve the cache. Above about 25 GB of weights there is little room left for one: Qwen3-32B (26.9 GB) tops out at 16k, and Seed-OSS-36B (29.7 GB) and Llama-3.3-70B (27.0 GB) at 8k, confirmed with flash attention forced on, so it is memory and not kernel choice. A bigger dense model costs speed and context at once.

Agents: real turns, several at once, and resuming

An agent at 256k is practical. The server keeps the context between turns, several conversations can share the cards, and a saved context reloads in seconds.

Eight real turns through llama-server

Each model got a real session with its own chat template. It read a document of about 234,000 tokens (119,000 for the 128k models) with eight facts planted at depths from 5% to 95%. 09

Method and limits

How the numbers were taken, and what they do not show.

How it was measured

  • Hardware. Two Tesla P100 16 GB cards, passively cooled in a desktop case, on an i5-10600K with card 1 on PCIe 3.0 ×4. Every weight and the whole KV cache lived in VRAM; nothing was offloaded to system RAM.
  • Software. llama.cpp b10970, CUDA 12.4 build, driver 582.78.
  • llama-bench (Method: bench). tg128 is writing 128 tokens; pp512 and pp2048 are reading 512 and 2,048 tokens. -d fills the cache to the stated depth first, untimed. 5 repetitions on an empty cache, 3 to 32k and 2 beyond; standard deviations at depth were 0.00–0.82 tok/s.
  • One-fill server climb (Method: server). llama-bench refills from zero for every test and repetition, which would have meant several three-hour fills for one million-token cell. So one llama-server filled the context once and climbed, reading 512 then 2,048 new tokens and writing 128 at each level (-ctxcp 0 --cache-ram 0, prompts sent as token IDs). Back to back on warm cards at 32k it agreed with llama-bench within 3% for reading 512 tokens (303.5 against 311.5 ± 7.4) and 6% for 2,048 (445.3 against 418.5). It is a single measurement, and the server caps a conversation at the model's trained context, so its top levels sit up to 4% below the llama-bench depths they stand in for (259,392 for 261,120; 128,320 for 130,048 and 131,072; 62,784 for Olmo's 65,536).
  • Other tools. llama-server for the fills, agent sessions, sustained-load runs and speculative decoding; llama-batched-bench for concurrency; llama-perplexity on wikitext-2 test (60 chunks at 512 context) for perplexity. GPU telemetry was sampled once a second throughout.

What the numbers do not show

  • Quality, mostly. Perplexity is compared only within the Qwen family, whose token IDs were verified identical. LFM2.5 and gemma-4 tokenize differently and are excluded. The Ornith-1.5 and gemma-4-12B runs aborted, and Qwen3.8-27B Q8_0 ran out of memory. Nothing measures quality for the other models, including the two IQ1_M models at 119B and 122B. "Good for agents" here means fast enough, not good.
  • The recall test is a floor. Eight exact six-digit strings, no reasoning needed. 8/8 is not a quality ranking; gemma's 5/8 is the informative result. Misses that came from a model spending its whole 1,024-token reply on reasoning (one for gpt-oss, two for gemma with reasoning on) are harness limits.
  • Heat. Long runs are heat-soaked, steady-state numbers: the right measure for agent work, pessimistic for one short prompt on an idle machine. Nemotron read its 32k–64k step at 554 tok/s from cold against 469 heat-soaked; by 256k the two were within 2%. Single-card runs used card 0 only, and that the one-card deficit is thermal is inferred from telemetry and one cool-card rerun.
  • Fill times in section 6 add up the steps (which sum to the final context within 30 tokens). They run 6% (Qwen3.6) to 12% (Nemotron) slower than one cold read of the same length in section 7, probably from heat.
  • Tensor split results are from one build; five models fail to load, one with an internal assertion, and that may change. At depth, tensor figures from the last round are paired with layer figures from earlier ones, so they include drift in card temperature; the gains are 2–10× the gap between methods. Empty-cache VRAM for the comparison covers both modes.
  • Context ceilings are for an f16 cache on both cards with layer split, one quant per model: Qwen3.6-35B-A3B reaches 256k at UD-Q4_K_M and 96k at UD-Q6_K.
  • Smaller points. Speculative-decoding figures are one request per workload. Some depth ladders stop at 64k because the plan did; the one real failure is DeepSeek-R1-Distill at 64k. Mistral-Small-4's ~6.5B active parameters is the published figure. Nemotron-Super-49B's failure is explained from buffer sizes, not traced in code. Concurrency runs conversations in lockstep, so a real server would see somewhat less. Reloads were measured with the file probably still in the OS file cache. The tokenizer figures come from one English and one code sample.
  • Missing cells. Nemotron reading 2,048 tokens at 1,047,552 is excluded: the re-run followed an unplanned restart that left cooling degraded (idle at 56 °C against 43 °C before; 458 and 681 MHz under load against 932 and 1,283), so its rates were 2–3× too low. Four more runs failed, leaving 14 empty cells: DeepSeek-R1-Distill reading 2,048 tokens at 0–32k (a CUDA error), and three Qwen3-Next-80B runs that exited at start.
